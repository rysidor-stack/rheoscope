How the factory works

A trust machine: it exists so you never have to take anyone's word, including its own.

This page starts with the part that matters most if you've never done this before: how to stand the factory up from nothing, and what actually running it day to day looks like. After that comes the full map of the factory itself (every station, how it works, and why), plus what the numbers look like once a venture has been running on it for a while.

Part 1

Standing it up from nothing

Written for someone who's never done this before: unpacking the package to having your first real knowledge filed and organized, in order, with the two moments that actually need your hands called out plainly.

1.1What you need before you start

  • A computer. Windows, Mac, or Linux all work.
  • A Claude account with Claude Code. If you don't already have it installed, just ask Claude itself to walk you through installing it. That's a completely normal thing to ask, the same way you'd ask a person for help setting up a new phone. You don't need to know anything technical going in.
  • A handful of small helper tools. The factory's own built-in Inspector checks for these automatically once you're set up, and tells you in plain English if anything's missing and how to get it. You don't need to track them down yourself.
One small practical tip Unpack the folder somewhere ordinary, like directly under Documents. Any normal location works fine; the one edge case is Windows and extremely deeply nested folders, and if you ever manage to hit it, setup stops with a clear message telling you exactly what to do rather than failing strangely.

1.2The setup, step by step

You never need a technical command for any of this. Open Claude Code in the folder, and tell it what you want in plain English. It handles the rest.

Unpack the package into a folder

You'll be handed the factory as a package. Unpack it into its own folder on your computer, somewhere shallow (see the tip above). That folder is now the venture's home.

Open Claude Code in that folder

Point Claude Code at the folder you just unpacked. It's the same as opening any app on a specific project: you're just telling it "work here."

Say "set this up for me"

Your own words are fine. Claude Code will ask a few basic questions first: the venture's name, a one-line description, who's involved. Then it configures itself and runs its own setup checks.

Human moment 1 Partway through, it will ask permission to turn on its built-in safety fence: the guardrail that blocks dangerous actions before they can run (more in Part 3). Say yes. This is the one setup question worth pausing on; everything else is safe to move through quickly.
Human moment 2 Claude Code may also pop up its own permission prompts along the way, asking to read or write a particular file, for instance. Normal, expected checkpoints. Approve them as they come.

The founding interview

Once setup finishes, you'll have a longer, unhurried conversation: not a building session, just talking. The part that matters most; its own section follows.

The health check comes back green

At the end of setup, the factory checks itself over automatically and tells you plainly whether everything is wired correctly. If you see one stray complaint about a deeply-nested test folder on Windows, that's the same long-path quirk from above, not a real problem. The fix is identical.

Drop in what you already have

Existing notes, research, documents: drop the files in and say "here's what I already have, please organize this into the wiki." That's your first real piece of organized knowledge, filed and cross-linked, on day one.

How long this actually takes

About an hour, start to finish: nearly all of it the founding interview, not the technical setup. Setup itself is usually a few minutes; the interview is deliberately unhurried, and rushing it is the one mistake worth avoiding.

1.3The founding interview

A real conversation, not a form. What you say here becomes the venture's constitution and its roadmap: the document every future session and every future decision gets checked against.

What it asks

  • What the venture is: in your own words, how the pieces fit together, not a feature list.
  • Where it's going: what "done" or "successful" actually looks like.
  • Hard constraints: the "thou shalt not" list, the kind you could actually catch happening.
  • The phases of the road ahead: three to five stretches of work, in order.
  • What kinds of knowledge it will hold: the subject areas the wiki organizes around.
  • Existing research or materials: anything you already have, and why it matters.
  • The handful of terms worth pinning down: words defined once, clearly, so nobody argues about them later.
  • Any hard-to-reverse decisions already made: the kind that would surprise someone joining later without the backstory.

How to answer well

Honest and specific, in your own words. If a question stops you cold (especially "what is this, architecturally" or "what does done look like"), that's a real signal, not a formality to push through: the venture hasn't settled into a shape yet, and a shallow-but-fast answer now becomes an anchor everything downstream gets measured against.

Better to pause and come back once you actually know the answer. Nobody's timing you.

1.4Green light: first knowledge in

Once the interview is done and the health check is green, the venture is genuinely live: its own constitution, its own roadmap, its own glossary, and (if you dropped anything in at step 6) its first organized, cross-linked knowledge already on file. Running it is Part 2.

Part 2

How you actually run it

A short daily habit, plus a small set of things you say out loud to move work forward. Nothing here needs to be memorized.

2.1The planning altitude stack

Three layers, from the months-long view down to today's work:

The Roadmap: born in the founding interview, this is the months-long map of where the venture is going, in phases. Changes rarely, and only on purpose.
/preflight Before committing to any phase or plan, the machine stress-tests it against everything it already knows. Requirements get challenged before work starts, not discovered as a failure after.
/flight-plan The start-of-session ritual: one briefing covering what changed, what's healthy, and what needs attention. The cockpit, where every session begins.

2.2The levers you actually pull

You don't memorize commands. Plain English routes to the right machinery on its own; these are shortcuts, never a requirement.

/flight-planBrings you up to speed on where things stand before you start working.
/compileTurns whatever raw notes are waiting into organized, linked knowledge.
/sweepChecks everything at once and gives you one plain-English health briefing.
/preflightStress-tests a plan against the evidence before you commit to it.
/orientAnswers any question about how your own project works, from your actual files, never from memory.
/doctorChecks that the machine itself (its tools, its wiring) is set up right.

2.3A week in the life

A concrete, ordinary week: the operator's actual touchpoints, and what the machine was doing underneath them at the same time.

Monday
Open the laptop, read the one-page briefing (about a minute). Two items are sitting in the decision inbox; both come with a recommendation attached, so you answer both in a couple of minutes and move on with your day.
Tuesday
Nothing needed. Everything ran clean overnight. You don't open the briefing at all, and nothing is lost by skipping it: it'll still be there tomorrow, current as of last night.
Wednesday
You drop in some new meeting notes and say "compile these." A couple of minutes of your time; the organizing work happens on its own afterward.
Thursday
One item in the inbox is a genuine business call, not a quick yes/no; you think about it over the day and answer that evening. Everything else in the briefing is clean.
Friday
A quick check-in, everything green, done for the week.

Meanwhile, the same week

Every one of those nights, the machine ran its full health check on its own, kept the wiki current, watched every deadline and renewal clock still years out, and logged every new piece of raw material with a full paper trail back to its source, whether or not you ever opened the laptop.

Total time you actually spent that week: well under half an hour. That gap between a week of real work and minutes of your attention is the entire point.

2.4What you never do

Reading code. Running anything by hand. Checking whether something "actually worked" yourself. The receipts and the briefings exist for exactly one reason: so you never have to run on a feeling about whether the factory did its job. You check the briefing, not your gut.

Part 3

The floor plan, in depth

Everything above is the practice. This part is the map underneath it: what each piece actually is, mechanically, and why it's built the way it is.

3.1The Spec Department

Provenance without manifests is documented ambiguity; manifests without provenance are unauditable assertion. The template ships both or it ships neither.

In plain English: knowing where every fact came from (Provenance, the paper trail: every fact chains to a dated source) and knowing what every build was supposed to do (Manifests: behavior written down before the build) are only trustworthy together. Records without specs leave you documented confusion; specs without records leave you claims you can't check. The paper trail itself is Part 4.1.

What it does

Before anything gets built, its required behavior is written down as a spec sheet made of individually checkable rows: discrete, testable facts, not a paragraph of prose someone has to interpret.

How many kinds of spec

The registry behind this holds twenty kinds of spec in total. Six are core, and every project starts with them: how it looks, how it behaves when clicked, its rules, its data, who may do what, and how it connects to other systems. Five more earned their place because something real went wrong without them, among them the format layer this factory's own daily briefing is certified under, plus what happens when something breaks, background and timed behavior, data migrations, and two people acting on the same thing at once. Nine more are named and waiting, each with its risk written down, activated only when a project first pays that risk's cost: performance budgets, logging, protecting sensitive fields, hardware, other languages, code structure rules, dependency upgrades, real-content fit, and loaded business settings.

The gate is mechanical, not a habit

The build machinery physically refuses to start work on anything that touches a piece of the product without a spec sheet for it already in place. Skipping it isn't something a rushed session can just forget to do.

"Done" becomes a count

After building, the rows get replayed against the real thing one by one. The result is a number, "how many of the rows actually pass," never "looks done." A surface that passes every row gets certified. This factory's own daily briefing is itself a certified surface: an 18-row spec sheet, all 18 currently passing. Two more surfaces already have their spec sheets extracted and their rows drafted, next in line for certification; only the first is certified today, deliberately, because certifying a surface is real work, not a rubber stamp.

Specs never rot quietly

When reality changes, the spec sheet is amended in writing, in the same breath as everything else that change invalidates, never a quiet edit nobody else sees.

Calibrated by a real experiment

The discipline isn't a guess. The same certified surface was built twice, independently, by two sessions working only from its spec sheet, then diffed against each other. The builds disagreed in two places the spec sheet hadn't actually pinned down (about one row in nine), and both gaps were written into the spec sheet as a result. The method catching its own blind spots, on the record.

Why it matters

"It works" becomes an audited count. Change becomes a declared amendment with a paper trail, never silent drift.

3.3The stations, in depth

What each main station does, how it works, and the failure it prevents.

Station 1

Intake Dock

What / How

Every raw thing that comes in (a session write-up, an export, a decision made out loud) lands as its own dated, labeled file in a running order that only ever grows. A fresh instance starts empty; nothing filed here is ever overwritten.

Why

A chat transcript evaporates; a shared drive silently overwrites. The dock exists so raw material is never lost or silently changed: the original words are always there to check a later conclusion against.

The one thing that needs a human hand

A session's own work can also propose brand-new knowledge, not just file what arrived from outside. But a machine-made proposal is never treated as your word. It sits tagged as proposed until you personally promote it with your own signing key, a step only human hands can take. Machine proposes, human signs; the filing system itself refuses to let a proposal quietly pass as something you confirmed.

Station 2

The Refinery

What / How

Turns what's waiting into an organized, cross-linked wiki: the only station allowed to change what it says. Every new sentence records exactly which raw file it came from. A fresh instance starts with the subject areas set at kickoff and grows its page count from there.

Why

Without it, "what we know" lives only in whoever was in the room. The refinery makes it one checkable, linked document instead of competing memories.

Station 3

The Quality Lab

What / How

Runs every health check nightly, read-only by construction (physically handed no tool that could change anything), and sorts findings into what's fine, what needs a decision, and what's being watched on purpose.

Why

Replaces chasing five tools by hand, or assuming things are fine because nothing's screamed lately, with one page and one glance.

Alongside Station 3

The Inspector

What / How

Checks the machine's own wiring (tools, safety hooks, every checker's own self-test), not the knowledge it produces. A clean nightly briefing only means something if the Inspector's own report is clean too.

Why

A checker can pass its own self-test while the thing it's supposed to watch was never actually connected: a green light proving only that the light works. That exact failure already happened here once, caught during a self-review.

Station 4

The Front Desk

What / How

The daily briefing and decision inbox. Every item here already survived being something the machine genuinely couldn't resolve alone: a plain yes/no, with what's wrong, what happens if ignored, and whether the fix needs your hands or just your word.

Why

Without one funnel, "what needs me today" means chasing a dozen checks yourself. The desk gives that question exactly one answer, never longer than it needs to be.

3.4The standing stations

Standing capabilities, not steps every piece of work passes through, with the receipts to prove they work.

Standing station

The Second-Opinion Desk

What it does

Important claims ("this is done," "this decision is safe") get independently challenged by a genuinely different company's AI before they're accepted, on one of three rungs matched to the stakes.

Three rungs

Quick opinion: one claim, one independent reviewer, minutes. Managed loop: interlocking claims get challenged, fixed, and re-challenged until nothing new turns up or a round cap hits; hitting the cap isn't failure, it's the signal to stop and write down what's still unsettled instead of papering over it. Full handoff: for the biggest calls, a case file argued across separately-briefed sessions on different AI substrates, ending in one locked decision record with its own confidence audit. Once locked, the venture never re-litigates it.

The receipts

Over the life of a mature instance, independent reviewers routinely send first-pass work back for a real fix rather than rubber-stamping it, better than one in three on the record so far. The multi-round rung earns its keep the same way: a hard decision that clears round one clean is the exception, not the rule; the normal case is a genuine defect caught, fixed, and confirmed, sometimes with a further round finding more before the review budget is spent.

Standing station

The Vault

What / How

Every password and key lives in your computer's own locked credential store, never in a plain file. A session asks for one by name; the vault hands it straight to the tool that needs it, never through the session itself.

Why

A credential in a config file or a chat is a leak waiting to happen. The vault removes the leak path instead of trusting everyone downstream to be careful.

Standing station

The Security Fence

What / How

A hard block on the specific actions that would be expensive or impossible to undo (network sends, mass deletions), enforced before anything runs, no matter who's asking. It has already blocked its own maintainer's change more than once, until the wording complied.

Why

Not a false alarm: the fence refusing to make an exception for its own builder is exactly the job. A safety system that trusts its maintainer to know when to bypass it isn't one.

Standing station

The Night Shift

What / How

Built, tested, ready housekeeping that would run the nightly checks, refresh the inbox, and draft a knowledge update on its own, overnight. It's rehearsed against real crash scenarios and writes only to a disposable draft; nothing becomes real until you say yes.

Why

New automation earns trust by being watched, not installed. Deliberately off until you've watched its first live run. Ready and turned on are two different things, on purpose.

Standing station

The Blueprint Store

What / How

The factory design itself, packaged so a new venture gets the whole thing (dock, refinery, lab, desk, fence, vault, spec sheets) from a standing start. Part 1 above is what going through it actually looks like.

Why

Building this fresh for every venture would burn exactly the attention the whole system exists to protect. One proven design makes the sixth venture as cheap as the first.

Part 4

The full picture

How the factory remembers over years, adapts to ventures of very different sizes, and the principles every piece above answers to.

4.1Long-horizon memory

Built to track work over months and years. A fresh instance starts at zero on every count below; what matters is that each one only ever grows, and grows honestly.

Every fact carries its own paper trail

An automatic count re-derives, from scratch, where every filed piece stands, and refuses to report "clean" unless every single one is accounted for: absorbed, waiting, set aside, or skipped, with none simply missing. That count runs every time, not just when something feels off.

A register that watches clocks years out

Certificate rotations, renewal windows, anything with a deadline years out: once entered, it's visible in every nightly briefing from that day forward, so "later" never quietly becomes "forgotten."

Sensors built to fail loud, not silent

A staleness check that only ever reports clean because it quietly crashed instead of flagging a real gap is worse than no check at all. This one is built the other way: fail loud, name the gap, and let a person close it, rather than let a crash pass for a clean bill of health.

The wiki compounds instead of evaporating

A research session six months out builds directly on everything already established. The wiki is a permanent asset; a chat transcript is not.

What this looks like at full scale

The one-line version: the equivalent of a 9,100-page technical encyclopedia, about 2.4 Harry Potter series, written, audited, and used to build and run real software.

4.2Adaptivity

One factory, dialed to what each venture actually needs and what's actually at stake.

Each venture opts into only what it needs

A research venture turns on the knowledge engine; one that ships software adds the code-quality module. Governance, the decision firewall, and the security fence are never optional. They're identical in every instantiation.

Verification scales with stakes, not habit

A payment-handling change gets the heaviest treatment: built by one process, checked by a completely separate one. A cosmetic display change can go through a lighter, self-checked pass. But that exemption is always written down, never just assumed.

One package, very different scales

The same package that grows into hundreds of tracked pieces, dozens of cross-linked wiki pages, and a real history of certified spec checks and independent reviews at full scale (Part 4's proof-of-scale figures above) could equally stand up a much smaller single-phase build. Same factory; only what's switched on changes.

4.3Why it's built this way

Four principles. Every piece above is downstream of one of them.

Your attention is the scarce part

You are very likely running more than one venture at once. Only genuine decisions are allowed to reach you: everything the machine can settle on its own, it settles on its own.

Trust nothing that hasn't been tested

"It's done" is never taken on faith: replayed against a spec or independently challenged, either way with a receipt. If it can't be checked, it isn't done yet.

Nothing deletes itself

History only grows. Deletion is never the machine's own decision, only yours, on purpose.

New automation has to earn its keys

Anything new ships switched off until you've personally watched it work. The Night Shift is sitting in exactly that waiting state right now.

Where things stand on a fresh instance

Shipped This template ships built and ready to run: every station in Part 3, wired and working.
Waiting The Night Shift ships off by default: built and tested, but switched off until you've personally watched its first supervised run. Once you have, arming it for good is a one-line request.
Dormant The multi-venture rollup desk (one glance across every venture at once) stays dormant until several ventures are actually running side by side; it needs that many to be worth showing.
